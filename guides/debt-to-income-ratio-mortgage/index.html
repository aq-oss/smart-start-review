Debt to income, and the maths behind a mortgage.

The ratio lenders use, the difference between the two versions of it, and the honest way to choose between paying off debt and saving for the deposit.

In short

  • Debt to income compares your monthly obligations with your monthly income before tax.
  • Lenders publish their own limits, and programs differ. There is no universal number.
  • Whether to pay debt or save depends on which of the two is actually blocking you.

(01)The ratio

What the number is, and where it comes from.

Add up what you must pay every month. Divide it by what you earn every month before tax. That percentage is your debt to income ratio.

Monthly income before tax5,000 (example)
Monthly obligations1,900 (example)

An illustration with round numbers, chosen to show the shape of the sum. It is not a target, a threshold or a recommendation.

1,900 divided by 5,000 is 38 percent. That is the back end ratio.

Front end

Housing costs alone against your income. The mortgage payment, the taxes, the insurance.

Back end

Everything you owe monthly against your income. Housing plus the car, the cards, the student loans and anything else with a minimum payment.

The limits

Each lender sets its own, and different loan programs allow different ceilings. Any single percentage quoted as the rule is somebody simplifying somebody else’s policy.

(02)The trade off

Pay off debt, or save for the deposit?

There is no universal answer, but there are only three situations. Work out which one you are in.

01

Expensive debt is the problem

A balance costing you a high rate every month is taking money that could be doing either job. Clearing it usually wins, because the return is certain.

02

The ratio is the problem

You have the deposit but the monthly obligations are too high for the payment you want. Reducing a monthly commitment moves the ratio.

03

The cash is the problem

Your ratio is comfortable but there is not enough set aside to close. Saving is the job, and clearing a low cost debt early does not help you.

This is a way of thinking about the decision, not financial advice, and it does not account for your own circumstances.
